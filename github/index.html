Why a leak can continue after rain stops

Rain does not always pass straight through a roof. Water can enter at one point and travel along the roof framing. It can sit in underlayment. It can sit in wood decking. It can sit in insulation. Then it can drip later, after the sky has cleared.

A shingle roof is a stack of layers. The wood deck forms the base. Underlayment sits over the deck. Shingles overlap from the bottom edge upward. When shingles wear, rain reaches the underlayment. Underlayment is a backup layer. It was not made to take weather by itself for a long time. If water passes it, the water reaches the wood deck. Wood can absorb water and stay damp for days. That damp wood can release water slowly, long after rain ends.

That pattern fits the report in this case. The supplied facts confirm the next step. Worn shingles had allowed water to damage the wood roof decking beneath them.

What was found

Shingles wear with age, sun, wind, and heat. Granules wear off the surface. The asphalt below becomes dry and brittle. Tabs can crack, curl, or lift. Wind can tear a tab off completely. A missing shingle leaves underlayment exposed. A cracked shingle opens a path for water.

In this case, worn shingles allowed water to damage the wood roof decking beneath them. The decking is the wood base that everything is fastened to. When decking gets wet and stays wet, it can soften. Soft wood cannot hold nails the same way. The shingles were the entry point. The decking was the layer that took the damage.

Why the decking had to be replaced

A repair that covers wet decking is not a repair. New shingles over soft wood can fail early. Nails can lose grip. The new surface can sit unevenly. Water can stay trapped below the new shingles. The leak can return.

Decking replacement removes the damaged wood. It gives the new shingles a solid base. It also lets the repair check the underlayment in that area. That principle explains why the work here went beyond a shingle swap. The problem had reached the wood below.

Lessons for homeowners

  • Note the timing of a leak. Write down when it appears, during rain, after wind, or after rain stops.
  • Photograph stains and drips. Mark the edge of a stain in pencil, with the date.
  • Look at the roof from the ground. Check for missing shingles, bare patches, and lifted tabs. Do not climb on the roof.
  • Report new sounds. Cracking sounds from roof wood are worth noting, even when the cause is not clear.
  • Ask what was found under the surface. A repair should check the underlayment and the wood deck, not only the shingles on top.
  • Keep records of earlier roof work. Dates, photos, and receipts help trace a leak that keeps coming back.
  • Do not paint over a stain as a fix. Fix the source first, then dry the area, then repaint.
  • Check the roof after a windy or stormy day. Photograph damage before anything is moved.
