Roof Decking and Plywood Damage: What It Is, How It Happens, and When It Needs to Be Replaced in Sunnyvale

A roof leak is often treated as a surface problem. A shingle is missing, a seal has failed, or a vent collar has cracked. The fix looks simple from the ground. The problem below the surface can be larger.

Roof decking sits below the shingles. It is the wood base that holds the roof together. When water reaches it and stays there, the wood can soften and lose strength. That damage can keep a roof leaking after rain stops. It can also make a new surface fail early, if the damaged wood is covered over instead of replaced.

This guide explains what roof decking is, how water damages it, how damage is found, and when replacement is needed. It is written for homeowners in Sunnyvale, California. For the main roofing resource, see Roof Repair Sunnyvale CA.

What roof decking is

Roof decking is the wood layer that covers the roof framing. It is also called sheathing. It forms a flat, solid base. Underlayment goes over it. Shingles go over the underlayment. Every nail, every shingle, and every flashing piece is fastened to the decking, directly or through the layers above it.

Plywood is a common decking material. It is made from thin sheets of wood glued together, with the grain of each layer running in a different direction. That structure gives it strength across a wide area. Other wood panels can serve the same role on some roofs. This guide uses the term decking for the wood base in general, and plywood decking where plywood is the material in question. It does not claim a specific plywood thickness for any home, because thickness was not supplied for a general guide, and it varies by roof.

Decking has three jobs. First, it gives the roof a solid surface to build on. Second, it holds fasteners in place. Third, it helps the roof carry weight, including the weight of the roofing, snow load where it applies, and a person working on the roof. When decking is sound and dry, it does those jobs well. When decking is wet and soft, it does them less well.

How a roof keeps water out, layer by layer

A shingle roof is a stack of layers, and each layer has a job. The wood deck forms the base. Underlayment sits over the deck. Shingles overlap from the bottom edge upward, so water runs over each one and onto the next. Sealant strips bond one shingle to the next. Granules shield the asphalt from the sun.

Water should stay on the outside of that stack. It should run down the shingles and off the roof edge. A failure in one layer can pass water to the next layer. A missing shingle leaves underlayment exposed. A cracked shingle opens a path for water. A lifted tab lets wind drive water under the shingle above it. Underlayment is a backup layer. It was not made to take weather by itself for a long time. If water passes the underlayment, it reaches the wood deck.

That is the point where a surface problem becomes a decking problem. The shingles may look like the only damage from the ground. The deck below may already be wet.

How water damages decking

Water damages decking by soaking into the wood and staying there. Dry wood is strong. Wet wood is softer. Wood that stays wet for days or weeks can lose strength, and it can start to break down. The damage is often hidden, because the deck is covered by underlayment and shingles on top, and by insulation and drywall below.

Several paths can let water reach the deck. Worn shingles are one path. Age, sun, wind, and heat wear shingles down. Granules wear off the surface. The asphalt below becomes dry and brittle. Tabs can crack, curl, or lift. Flashing at a chimney, wall, or valley can lift or lose its seal. A vent pipe collar can crack in the sun. A valley can collect debris and hold water. An exposed nail can back out and open a path for water. Each of these is a general cause, not a claim about a specific home.

Time matters. A short leak may wet only the surface. A leak that goes on for days, or that repeats with each rain, can soak the deck. Sunnyvale has long dry months and rainy winters. Roofs wear quietly through summer. Weak spots show up in the first heavy storms. A roof that never leaked before can drip in the first big rain of the season. That is general guidance about the local rain cycle, not a claim about the weather in a specific case.

Signs of damaged decking

Decking damage is hard to see from the ground, because the deck is covered. Signs usually show up inside the home, in the attic, or on the roof surface. No single sign proves decking damage. Each sign is a reason to look further.

Inside the home, a ceiling stain is a common sign. Brown, yellow, or tan marks, often with a darker ring at the edge, point to water. A ring that grows after rain points to an active leak. Paint that bubbles, peels, or cracks near the ceiling can point to the same problem. A musty smell in a room or in the attic, especially after rain, can point to damp wood or insulation.

In the attic, the signs are more direct. Damp or flattened insulation, dark wood, and water trails on the underside of the roof point to water. Daylight showing through the roof boards, when looking up from inside the attic, points to a gap in the roof. A stain that shows up only after rain, or only in wind-driven rain, usually points to the roof, not to plumbing.

On the roof surface, missing, curled, or cracked shingles point to a possible entry point. Shingle pieces in the gutters point to wear. Granules in the gutters point to surface wear. A roof that dips between supports, or along the ridge, is a different sign. A dip can point to long-term water softening the deck and framing. Patching a leak will not straighten a sag. A new sag is a reason to call sooner, and to have the framing inspected.

Sounds can also be a sign worth noting. Cracking sounds from roof wood were reported in one Central Sunnyvale case, and the cause was not documented in that case. Sounds need an inspection. A sound alone does not name a cause, and it does not prove structural failure.

How decking damage is found

Decking damage is found by working backward from the sign, not by guessing from the surface. Water follows roof framing downhill, sometimes several feet, before it drops onto a ceiling. The entry point is usually higher than the stain.

A general inspection follows a simple order. Questions come first. Where is the stain, when does it appear, and has the roof been repaired before. An attic check follows. The underside of the roof is checked for wet or stained wood, damp insulation, and water trails. The trails are followed uphill. A roof surface check follows. The area above the stain is checked, including flashing, vents, valleys, fasteners, and damaged roofing.

A water test may follow, when the cause is not obvious. One section of roof is wetted at a time, while someone watches inside to see which section leaks. A moisture meter can confirm how far water has spread. That order is general guidance. It is not a description of the inspection in the Washington Park case, because the inspection steps for that case were not supplied.

A leak fixed without checking the attic or the area above the stain is a guess. A homeowner can ask how the source was confirmed.

What happens if damaged decking is left in place

Covering damaged decking is not a repair. New shingles over soft wood can fail early. Nails can lose grip. The new surface can sit unevenly. Water can stay trapped below the new shingles. The leak can return.

Wet wood can also affect the home below the roof. Wet insulation loses its ability to insulate. Drywall that sags, crumbles, or stays damp usually has to be cut out and replaced. Wet insulation and wood that stay damp can grow mold. If mold is seen or smelled, or the wet area is large, the area needs to be checked before the ceiling is closed up. That is general guidance, not a finding about a specific home.

The longer wet decking stays in place, the more work the repair can become. A small area of wet decking can be replaced in a limited repair. A larger area, or a spot that keeps leaking after repairs, is a reason to discuss a wider repair, or replacement of the affected section. The decision depends on how much wood is damaged, and on the condition of the rest of the roof.

How decking replacement works, in general

Decking replacement follows a simple order, in general. The roofing over the damaged area is removed. Debris is cleared. The damaged wood is removed. New decking is installed in its place. New underlayment and new roofing go over the repaired decking. Fasteners are covered, seams are sealed, and new material is lapped so water runs off the way it should.

That order is general guidance. It matches the four supplied steps in the Washington Park case, and it is presented here as a general process, not as a claim about every repair. The exact scope depends on the area of damage, the roof type, and what is found under the surface. No plywood thickness, sheet count, or decking area is given in this guide, because none was supplied for a general guide.

Drying matters too. A repair stops new water. It does not dry a ceiling or a wall. Drywall with only a stain can often be covered with a stain-blocking primer and fresh paint, but only after the area is completely dry. Drywall that sags or crumbles should be replaced. Roofing and drywall are different trades, so a second contractor is often involved for the ceiling.

What to do first when a leak appears

Move furniture and belongings away from the wet area. Put a bucket under the drip. Take photos and a short video, and mark the edge of the stain in pencil with the date. Stay clear of wet ceiling light fixtures. If water is near wiring, turn off power to that room. Stay off the roof, especially when it is wet.

Write down when the leak shows up: during rain, after wind, or only after a long storm. That timing helps narrow the search. Photograph damage from the ground, before anything is moved or repaired. Keep any paperwork from earlier roof work, because the shingle brand and color can help with matching, and dates and photos help trace a leak that keeps coming back.

Do not paint over a stain as a fix. Do not smear sealant on the roof as a fix. Both can cover the symptom while water keeps getting in. Fix the source first, then dry the area, then repaint. If water is coming in heavily, ask about a temporary cover to hold the roof until a full repair. That is a question to ask, not a step to take alone.

Lessons for homeowners in Sunnyvale

  • Note the timing of a leak. A leak after rain stops points to water held in the roof layers.
  • Check the attic, or have it checked, for wet wood and water trails. The stain is rarely directly under the leak.
  • Ask what was found under the surface. A repair should check the underlayment and the wood deck, not only the shingles on top. Ask for photos.
  • Do not accept a surface fix over soft wood. New shingles over soft decking can fail early.
  • Keep records of earlier roof work. Dates, photos, and receipts show a pattern when a leak keeps coming back.
  • Check the roof from the ground after a windy or stormy day. Look for missing shingles, bare patches, and lifted tabs.
  • Treat a new sag, a growing stain, or water near wiring as a reason to call sooner.

FAQs

What is roof decking?

Roof decking is the wood layer that covers the roof framing. It forms a flat, solid base for underlayment and shingles. It holds fasteners in place, and it helps the roof carry weight. Plywood is a common decking material.

How does water damage decking?

Water soaks into the wood and stays there. Dry wood is strong. Wet wood is softer. Wood that stays wet for days or weeks can lose strength. The damage is often hidden, because the deck is covered on top and below.

Can decking be dried, or does it always need to be replaced?

It depends on how wet the wood is, and for how long. The live leak repair page states the rule in plain terms: if the underlayment or the wood deck is wet or soft, it needs to be dried or replaced. Covering it up is not a repair. An inspection can check whether the wood is still sound after drying.

Why did a roof keep leaking after rain stopped?

Water was held in the roof layers. Worn shingles let water reach the wood decking below. Wet decking can stay damp for days. It can release water slowly, after the sky has cleared. That pattern was documented in the Washington Park case linked above.

How long does a decking repair take?

It depends on the area of damage, and on what is found under the surface. A small area of wet decking can be part of a limited repair. A larger area takes more time. Roofing also needs dry weather, so rain can delay the work.
